Tesseract — the AI browser
Tesseract is an AI browser, not an AI assistant inside a browser. The distinction is the whole project. Google's AI mode is limited because everything in the context lives in the chat — it can't actually use the browser to go and get what you want. The complaint that started this was about access and friction, not reasoning: hunting a free transcript through locked archive pages, click, open, locked, again, "like a 2010s librarian sorting through an infinite digital library." So the moat isn't intelligence, which is bought by the token. It's access — this thing runs on your machine with your own logged-in session, and it reads what you can already see. Where the URL bar would be, there's one field that takes what you want instead of where you want to go.
The design rule that governs everything is: build orthogonal primitives the model cannot invent, and never build the examples. A planner that pattern-matches "dark" to a restyle command is a demo wearing a capability's clothes. When the ask is "find who isn't following me back", the correct build isn't a follower-diff feature — it's read a list, scroll an infinite list, read another list, and let the model do the set difference in three steps. The division of labour: structures like video decoding are not parts the AI can supply, so they get built; the reasoning on top of them never gets hard-coded.
Lens is the reading engine underneath, and it is genuinely fast: an hour of video read in 3.77 seconds, 956× realtime, 98.9% recall, zero wrong — by decoding 1.7% of the file. That came out of four measurements. WebCodecs' VideoDecoder does 2,166 fps on 720p H.264, 136× the naive seek path, because nothing is ever presented — the <video> element's seek→decode→compositor→vsync pipeline exists to feed a human eye 60 fps, and we are not an eye. The decoded frame's Y plane already is the grayscale, so a whole conversion pass disappears. A P-frame's byte size tracks how much of the picture moved and those sizes are in the MP4 header, so on-screen text changes can be located with zero decoding — 111 candidate frames out of 9,000 held 91% of all caption changes. And keyframes decode independently, so at 2-second spacing an hour gives 1,800 free-standing frames, finer granularity than on-screen text ever changes at. Audio runs at 867× realtime, and a silence gate means 87% of a file is never sent to a recogniser at all. Three hundred videos read concurrently in 235 seconds — fifty hours of footage — because the reading layer never plays anything, so the shape is a queue with bounded workers, not a crowd of renderers.
Then "the whole internet" turned out to mean the formats it's made of. A browser that reads HTML text and MP4 is reading a slice of the web and calling it the web. So: a zero-dependency PDF reader (122 of 122 source sentences verbatim, 13 ms, tested against PDFs written by Chromium's own printer rather than by its author, so it faces real Flate streams, real subset fonts and real /ToUnicode maps), scanned-page OCR for roughly everything digitised before 2005, cross-origin iframe reading that no page's own JavaScript is allowed to do, and text read out of images — charts, screenshots, tables pasted as PNG. The joke of it: the code had literally been using "I have no way to read a PDF rendered inside a plugin viewer" as its stock example of a legitimate missing capability. It was the real blind spot, sitting in the source as a joke about itself.
Being a real browser rather than an extension is what buys all of it. Input goes through Chromium's own pipeline, so the page reads isTrusted === true — an extension needs chrome.debugger, which means a permanent warning banner and detaches the moment DevTools opens. Media is fetched in the main process with the user's own cookies, so a lecture behind a login reads like a public file, where an extension is still fighting CORS with <all_urls>. Restyling is insertCSS before first paint, remembered per origin, with no review queue. A Chromium fork would have cost a year and bought nothing on that list; Chromium as a library cost an afternoon and bought all of it. The chrome itself is redrawable by the model: one token vocabulary — ink, ground, surface, line, accents, radius, density, motion, glow — reaching all three surfaces, exposed as two orthogonal capabilities (ui.read() returns every token, its legal values and the live contrast ratios; ui.theme() sets any subset). There is deliberately no ui.dark(), because a preset per look can only do what someone thought of in advance. And a theme that would drop text contrast below 4.5:1 is refused with the ratio in the refusal, because {ink:'#000', ground:'#000'} is a plausible model turn and an unusable browser you can't see the settings button to undo.
What makes the agent affordable is a memory primitive, not a bigger model. The first version fenced every observation, in full, into every turn — prompt cost was O(steps × every byte ever read), so three ordinary pages hit 22k tokens by step four and twenty pages was a quarter of a million tokens per step. The "read three hundred videos" case the browser exists for wasn't slow, it was arithmetically impossible. Now bodies stay in the main process and the model gets a catalogue of handles plus three ways in — list, grep, read: 264,289 characters over five sources become 3,906 bytes of prompt per turn, 68× smaller, and a fact buried 37,227 characters into the third document is still found, quoted, and cited by its character offset. Independent reads batch into one turn — six of them in 211 ms rather than the 1,200 ms they cost one at a time. "Search the four megabytes I read three steps ago without paying to re-read it" is not something a model can assemble out of tabs and clicks.
The spine of the product is a doctrine: it may never say "I can't." Every agent product on the market fails the same way — it hits a wall and emits a sentence beginning "I'm unable to," and that's the moment the user stops believing it's a browser and remembers it's a chatbot with a URL bar. So every wall gets a ladder, built as a real primitive (attempt()) that runs fallback rungs in order and returns the first success annotated with how far down it had to go, so the model can tell the truth about the degradation. A paywall climbs to the print view, then AMP, then the OpenGraph card and abstract, then a citation of what it could see. A login wall and a bot check hand the keyboard back to you mid-task and resume — it will not type a credential and will not solve a CAPTCHA. DRM is named as a mechanism below the browser rather than a failure of it, and the captions and chapters around the player still come back. "I don't know" is legal; "I can't" is a bug with a bug number. The mirror of that is an honesty invariant: every answer carries a coverage line generated from a ledger rather than written by the model — "Read 3 of 5 sources," with the two it couldn't reach named and why — and the browser greps its own answer against what it actually read and tells you which sentences it could not confirm.
Security is three layers, because one check is one point of failure. Web content enters the model inside a nonce'd envelope it cannot close, labelled with its origin; it's scanned for instruction-shaped text before the model sees it, and what's found is reported to you verbatim rather than quietly stripped (an attacker who knows you strip writes around the filter, and a user who never learns the page attacked them can't make a good decision about that site); and a side-effecting step whose justification traces back to flagged content cannot execute, whatever the model says. Twelve injection channels detected, zero obeyed. The measurement that matters more, though, is the one nobody publishes: over 54 real websites, the false-positive rate at which the detector would block an ordinary page is 0%, down from 14.8% — because a detector that flags the GDPR and Jane Austen is one the user switches off, and then it's 0/12 forever.
And all of it is proved rather than asserted. 428 hermetic acceptance checks plus a live suite against 54 real websites — right-to-left and CJK scripts, a 2.8 MB government PDF, an entire novel, five paywalls, a redirect chain, a 404 and a dead host — where a correctly named wall counts as a pass and getting through a bot check would count as a failure. Runs are recorded to a cassette so a failure replays offline with the cable unplugged, and one command prints one verdict over both halves; with no network it falls back to the tape and says how old the tape is, and it will never print GREEN on the hermetic half alone. That last rule is not academic — the suite was green on the day perception took nine seconds on the HTML spec, on the day every control inside a web component was perceivable and unclickable, and on the day a wrong index made it delete the third row when you asked for the first. None of those are exotic. All three were invisible because the definition of green had no internet in it.
